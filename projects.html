SpaceX

Starship Booster Cryo-Proof Tooling

May – August 2026

I redesigned Super Heavy’s cryo-proof tooling to reduce leak paths and make installation repeatable. My work covered welded drain elbows, self-clocking blankoffs, a GOX seal interface, and assembly-level integration.

Explore this project: Booster cryo-proof tooling +

Identify weak interfaces

I reviewed the tooling sealing 33 engine feedlines for unnecessary leak joints and difficult installation steps. I replaced bolted interfaces with welded connections where practical, rerouted drain tubes for work access, and revised remaining bolted connections to use standard hardware.

Drain elbow: fewer leak paths

The drain-to-blankoff interface had a bolted stackup with several potential leak paths. I replaced it with a 90° elbow welded to the blankoff, leaving one B-nut joint to the flared drain tube through an AN connection with a copper seal. I chose 304 stainless steel to match the blankoff’s weld material.

Weld fit-up: locate and clock

Outlet orientation had to repeat across different blankoffs. I added a radial key and mating cutout to clock the elbow, plus a diameter step to locate its height for welding. I toleranced the fit to keep the TIG weld root gap within the specified 0.5 mm maximum.

Loads: size the weld and recheck the part

I sized the elbow weld using Blodgett’s method and the maximum outlet moment from tank-pressure blow-off loads. The clocking cutout changed the blankoff geometry enough to require new analysis, so I checked each revised blankoff in Ansys under internal pressure.

GOX port: weld and seal geometry

I eliminated another bolted leak interface by designing a Monel bung welded to a stainless blankoff. I toleranced the interface to minimize gaseous-oxygen (GOX) impingement risk and sized the dissimilar-metal weld using Blodgett’s method. The bung formed the inner lip of the upper seal gland, which I sized for the required O-ring compression using the Parker O-Ring Handbook.

Repeatability: testing and mistake-proofing

Empirical testing showed the Teflon O-ring was more consistent and reliable than the spring-energized alternative under evaluation. Elsewhere in the assembly, I redesigned a subtly asymmetric welded subassembly so it could not be installed incorrectly.

Carry the design into use

I evaluated manufacturing deviations against critical sealing requirements and oversaw welded-subassembly fabrication and vehicle integration. I maintained the 13,000-component CAD assembly across three vehicles at different release states, and designed lineside storage and material flow to support two vehicles using the tooling simultaneously.

SpaceX

Starship Production Tooling

January – August 2025

On SpaceX’s Structures Build Engineering team, I helped design and build a new weld-focused subassembly cell for a high-pressure propulsion manifold for Starship V3. My work included flange fit-up fixtures, lift and access tooling, flight-part retrofits, and production improvements.

Explore this project: Starship production tooling +

Weld cell: control flange fit-up

I helped design the manifold subassembly cell and designed self-clocking endpoint-flange fixtures. I used locating features and GD&T to control flange position and orientation for welding, then oversaw the cell’s integration on the production floor.

Lift tool: size and prove the load path

I designed a manifold lift tool for a 1,100 lb working load limit, using bending and weld calculations to size it. The tool was proof-tested at twice that limit, or 2,200 lb.

Torque frames: improve work access

I designed an integration jig for Starship’s aft flap torque frames to improve technician access. I developed the assembly sequence and used integration loads in bending and torsion to size the base, plates, and welds.

Flight-part retrofits

I designed five flight-part retrofits to accommodate off-nominal conditions in existing assemblies. I took precise measurements of the as-built hardware, created CAD models of those assemblies, and used them to define custom parts for manufacture and installation.

Production improvements

I also designed 3D-printed parts and parts manufactured through Protolabs to improve the build process.

Longhorn Racing Electric

Brake Rotors & Mounts

2025 – 2026

I designed the rotor and mount for reliable braking within the drivetrain package, combining material tradeoffs, thermal analysis, structural FEA, and temperature testing.

Explore this project: Brake rotors & mounts +

Materials: separate the thermal roles

I compared cast iron and 4140 steel and selected steel for its endurance-limit advantage. Titanium buttons limited heat transfer into the 7075 aluminum mount, which I packaged at the differential bolts.

Thickness: bound and refine the heat load

For the 500°C requirement, I first sized thickness assuming maximum braking energy became heat with no heat escaping. I refined that conservative estimate with transient conduction, forced convection, and radiation analysis.

Geometry: manage debris and check stress

I added curved face slots for debris removal, de-gassing, and water clearance, accepting additional pad wear. In Ansys, I supported the pad contact regions and applied braking moment through the mount connections to check stress.

Validation: measure operating temperature

I used temperature crayons to assess rotor operating temperatures and prepared the design for three-axis CNC manufacturing.

Longhorn Racing Electric

Rear Wheel Hub & Bearing Assembly

2024 – 2025

I designed a rear hub and bearing assembly that brought the drivetrain, wheel, and electronics interfaces together in a lightweight, manufacturable package.

Explore this project: Rear hub & bearing assembly +

Interfaces: establish the envelope

I defined the hub around the drivetrain’s tripod bearing and the electronics team’s Hall sensors. I screened bearings in SKF SimPro Quick for load capacity, fatigue life, and power loss within an 80–85 mm bore and 110–120 mm outer-diameter envelope; the selection set hub thickness and packaging.

Material: balance strength and mass

I compared titanium, 6061, and 7075 aluminum and selected 7075-T6. Its higher yield strength than 6061 supported the hub’s structural requirements.

Load model and thread sizing

I applied wheel loads from the team’s truss solver to the wheel flange, modeled the tensile load from the center-lock nut, and included bearing forces and drivetrain torque at the tripod interface. I sized hub-nut engagement using bolt tensile and nut thread-shear areas.

Geometry: remove mass, then check

I built the geometry around the interfaces and selected bearing, removed excess material through pocketing, and checked the revised hub in Ansys. Rotational features were turned first; CNC milling produced the tripod cutout, pin and button holes, and flange pockets.

Calculations & reference material +

BEARING SELECTION / PART OF THE HUB ASSEMBLY

Wheel bearing selection & analysis

I selected the SKF 71817 ACDGB/VQ253 angular-contact bearing after comparing candidates within the drivetrain packaging constraints. The SKF SimPro Quick study checked static and dynamic load capacity, fatigue life, and power loss across the operating wheel-speed range.

The selection saved 0.8 lb and established dimensions that fed into the hub design and thickness requirements. The simulation setup and power-loss plot are included in the project gallery.

Longhorn Racing Electric

Pushrods & Control Arms

2023 – 2024

I developed and fabricated a lighter suspension assembly, pairing carbon-fiber pushrods with steel control arms and checking both the bonded joints and on-car clearance.

Explore this project: Pushrods & control arms +

Materials: reduce mass within shop capability

I kept steel control arms because the team had greater confidence in that manufacturing process, and switched the pushrods to carbon fiber to reduce mass. Apex-mounted gussets replaced a heavy mounting plate.

Pushrod testing and bonding

I tested the carbon-fiber pushrods in tension and compression on an Instron. For fabrication, I cut the tubes to length and turned aluminum end plugs, controlling the bonding-surface diameter to within half a thousandth of an inch.

Fabrication: carry the geometry into hardware

I cut steel tubing, turned and milled end plugs, bored and reamed bearing seats in laser-cut plates, and welded and tapped the plugs for rod ends.

Integration and steering clearance

During freshman-year integration, a missed tolerance stackup caused the apex to interfere at maximum steer. We checked the required material removal in Ansys and ground the existing apex to recover clearance. The combined suspension redesign saved 4.6 lb.

Longhorn Racing Electric

Discrete Ride-Height Adjustment

2023 – 2024

I designed a shim-based attachment to make ride-height changes more repeatable, replacing a process that relied on turning an opposite-thread pushrod.

Explore this project: Discrete ride-height adjustment +

A discrete adjustment

The mechanism changes pushrod length by adding or removing shims and retightening the fasteners. The attachment connects to the inboard suspension mechanism and keeps the shim stack in compression.

Load path and geometry

I designed the attachment in 7075 aluminum and kept the shim pockets concentric with the retaining bolts. Lightweight shim geometry reduced the mass added by the adjustment mechanism.

Validation and tradeoff

I checked the attachment under pushrod loading in SolidWorks FEA. The shims were laser-cut and the attachment pieces machined; another team member CNC-machined the upper component. The mechanism added 0.7 lb across the car in exchange for repeatable adjustment.
