SpaceX

Starship Booster Cryo-Proof Tooling

May – August 2026

I redesigned cryo-proof test tooling to simplify assembly, reduce unnecessary leak paths, and improve test repeatability on Super Heavy.

Explore this project: Booster cryo-proof tooling +

A repeatable test setup

The inherited assembly had unnecessary leak paths, a complicated bolted stackup, and no reliable CAD reference. I rebuilt the assembly model as the design reference and used it to coordinate the tooling changes.

Simpler interfaces

I replaced the plate-to-drain bolted stackup with a custom welded elbow fitting that located its own orientation for welding. I also worked with the propulsion responsible engineer to revise the plate interfaces for high-flow gaseous-oxygen test tooling.

Hardware and load checks

I replaced specialized bolts with standard factory hardware and designed the tooling around the maximum pressure-induced separation loads from the vehicle tanks.

Build and integration

My responsibility extended through procurement, fabrication, and installation. The overall tooling program saved more than 1,100 production hours per vehicle.

SpaceX

Starship Production Tooling

January – August 2025

I designed the fixtures and handling tools that supported a Starship manifold build, from locating weld interfaces to lifting hardware and setting up repeatable assembly work.

Explore this project: Starship production tooling +

Weld-cell layout and locating

I explored station layouts in CAD, selected welding tables, and designed precision fixtures for the manifold’s endpoint flanges. Dowel pins, toggle clamps, and machined blocks established flange position and orientation using 3-2-1 locating principles and GD&T.

Manifold lift tooling

I designed a lift tool to position the manifold on the vehicle for integration. The work included bending and weld calculations, locating strategy, and critical-lift requirements. The tool was proof-tested at twice its working load.

Torque-frame integration jig

A new welded structure needed a repeatable place to receive its bolted components. I developed the assembly sequence and an integration jig at a comfortable working height, designed for repeated production use.

Sizing the jig

I used maximum integration loads in two-plane bending and torsion to size the base, integration plates, and welds.

Brake Rotors & Mounts

2025 – 2026

I designed the rotor and mount for reliable braking within the drivetrain package, combining material tradeoffs, thermal analysis, structural FEA, and temperature testing.

Explore this project: Brake rotors & mounts +

Packaging & requirements

I coordinated space claims with the powertrain team so the rotor would fit the drivetrain assembly. Long-term reliability was the main goal, with a design requirement to tolerate temperatures up to 500°C with a safety margin.

Rotor material selection

I compared cast iron and 4140 steel in a material decision matrix. The study weighed cast iron’s density and thermal-conductivity advantages against fatigue performance, and I selected steel for its endurance-limit advantage in that comparison.

Buttons & mount

I chose titanium buttons for their low thermal conductivity, strength-to-weight ratio, and corrosion resistance. They act as a thermal choke between the steel disc and the lightweight 7075 aluminum mount, which interfaces with the differential bolts.

Thermal sizing

I first sized rotor thickness using a conservative case in which maximum braking energy became heat with no heat escaping. I then refined the sizing through transient heat-transfer analysis, considering conduction, forced convection, and radiation.

Slot geometry & structural FEA

I used curved face slots for pad debris removal, de-gassing, and water clearance, accepting additional pad wear. In Ansys, I fixed the pad contact regions and distributed the maximum rules-based braking moment through the mount’s bolted connections to check stress states.

Temperature testing & manufacturing

I tested the rotors with temperature crayons to assess their operating temperatures. Manufacturing was outsourced for three-axis CNC machining.

Rear Wheel Hub & Bearing Assembly

2024 – 2025

I designed a rear hub and bearing assembly that brought the drivetrain, wheel, and electronics interfaces together in a lightweight, manufacturable package.

Explore this project: Rear hub & bearing assembly +

System interfaces

I collaborated with the drivetrain and electronics teams to define the assembly’s interfaces. The hub needed to accommodate the drivetrain’s tripod bearing and integrate the electronics team’s Hall effect sensors.

Bearing selection and material

I evaluated bearings in SKF SimPro Quick within an 80–85 mm inner-diameter and 110–120 mm outer-diameter envelope. The selection informed hub thickness and packaging. I compared titanium, 6061, and 7075 aluminum, selecting 7075-T6.

Load model and thread sizing

I applied wheel loads from the team’s truss solver to the wheel flange, modeled the tensile load from the center-lock nut, and included bearing forces and drivetrain torque at the tripod interface. I sized hub-nut engagement using bolt tensile and nut thread-shear areas.

Geometry and pocketing

I started with geometry that satisfied the system interfaces and bearing constraints, then removed excess material through pocketing and checked the revised hub in Ansys.

Manufacturing sequence

The rotational features were turned on a lathe first. CNC milling then produced the tripod cutout, dowel-pin holes, brake-button holes, and wheel-flange pockets.

Calculations & reference material +

BEARING SELECTION / PART OF THE HUB ASSEMBLY

Wheel bearing selection & analysis

I selected the SKF 71817 ACDGB/VQ253 angular-contact bearing after comparing candidates within the drivetrain packaging constraints. The SKF SimPro Quick study checked static and dynamic load capacity, fatigue life, and power loss across the operating wheel-speed range.

The selection saved 0.8 lb and established dimensions that fed into the hub design and thickness requirements. The simulation setup and power-loss plot are included in the project gallery.

PRELIMINARY DESIGN / MASS & PACKAGING

Sizing the hub and bearings together

We compared 50 mm and 60 mm bearing-bore options by combining the estimated hub mass with the inboard and outboard bearing weights. This made the effect of each packaging choice visible at the assembly level.

The comparison helped frame the design around total hub-and-bearing mass, load capacity, and available space. Reducing the hub’s mass alone would not establish that the complete assembly was lighter.

The source lists bearing 61912 at 0.69 lb in the upper comparison and 0.43 lb in the reference list. These preliminary totals are not used as verified final weight results.

Pushrods & Control Arms

2023 – 2024

I developed and fabricated a lighter suspension assembly, pairing carbon-fiber pushrods with steel control arms and checking both the bonded joints and on-car clearance.

Explore this project: Pushrods & control arms +

Interfaces and material choices

The control arms had to connect the upright, chassis tabs, and pushrods. I kept steel for the control arms, where the team had greater confidence in the manufacturing process, and used carbon fiber for the pushrods to reduce mass.

Pushrod testing and bonding

I tested the carbon-fiber pushrods in tension and compression on an Instron. For fabrication, I cut the tubes to length and turned aluminum end plugs, controlling the bonding-surface diameter to within half a thousandth of an inch.

Control-arm fabrication

I replaced a heavy mounting plate with gussets at the apex. I cut steel tubing, turned and milled end plugs, bored and reamed bearing seats in laser-cut plates, and welded and tapped the plugs for rod ends.

Integration and steering clearance

During freshman-year integration, a missed tolerance stackup caused the apex to interfere at maximum steer. We checked the required material removal in Ansys and ground the existing apex to recover clearance. The combined suspension redesign saved 4.6 lb.

Longhorn Racing Electric

Discrete Ride-Height Adjustment

2023 – 2024

I designed a shim-based attachment to make ride-height changes more repeatable, replacing a process that relied on turning an opposite-thread pushrod.

Explore this project: Discrete ride-height adjustment +

A discrete adjustment

The mechanism changes pushrod length by adding or removing shims and retightening the fasteners. The attachment connects to the inboard suspension mechanism and keeps the shim stack in compression.

Load path and geometry

I designed the attachment in 7075 aluminum and kept the shim pockets concentric with the retaining bolts. Lightweight shim geometry reduced the mass added by the adjustment mechanism.

Checks and manufacturing

I checked the attachment under pushrod loading in SolidWorks FEA. The shims were laser-cut and the attachment pieces machined. The upper component’s CNC machining was handled by another team member.

The tradeoff

The mechanism added 0.7 lb across the car in exchange for a more repeatable setup adjustment.
