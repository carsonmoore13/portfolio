Starship Booster Cryo-Proof Tooling

May – August 2026

I redesigned Super Heavy’s cryo-proof tooling to reduce leak paths and make installation repeatable: welded drain elbows, a GOX seal interface, and standard attachment bolts, carried from load calculations into vehicle integration.

Explore this project: Booster cryo-proof tooling +

  1. 01

    Problem statement

    The cryo-proof assembly had leak-prone interfaces, difficult work access, and opportunities for incorrect assembly. I redesigned the tooling sealing the booster’s 33 feedlines.

  2. 02

    Design requirements

    Withstand maximum feedline blow-off loads, control elbow orientation and weld fit-up, improve GOX sealing, and maintain bolt preload under repeated loading.

  3. 03

    Material selection

    I chose 304 stainless steel for the elbow to match the blankoff’s weld material. The GOX redesign used a Monel bung and stainless blankoff; load, gapping, and fatigue checks supported switching the attachment bolts to stocked Grade 5 hardware.

  4. 04

    Design & validation

    Drain elbow & blankoffs

    1. Replace the leak-prone stackup

      I replaced a leak-prone bolted joint stackup with a welded 90° elbow connecting the blankoff to the cryogenic drain tube. The elbow welded directly to the blankoff, leaving one B-nut joint at the flared tube’s AN connection with a copper seal. I chose 304 stainless steel to match the blankoff’s weld material.

    2. Size the vehicle-side weld, then control fit-up

      I used the maximum elbow outlet moment and loads from the cryo-proof feedline blow-off cases to size the elbow’s vehicle-side weld to the blankoff using Blodgett’s method. A radial key clocked the outlet; a diameter step located weld height. I toleranced the fit to keep the TIG root gap within 0.5 mm.

    3. Recheck the revised blankoffs

      I ran Ansys simulations on the revised blankoff geometries, including the new clocking cutouts. I applied pressure to their internal faces based on the maximum feedline blow-off load cases in the cryo-proof test.

    GOX port & seal

    1. Improve sealing at a faulty interface

      I designed a Monel bung welded to a stainless blankoff to improve sealing performance at another faulty interface. I controlled the interface geometry to minimize gaseous-oxygen (GOX) impingement risk and sized the dissimilar-metal weld using Blodgett’s method.

    2. Design the seal gland

      The bung protruded through the blankoff and sat against a counterbore in the blankoff to form the inner lip of an upper seal gland. I used the Parker O-Ring Handbook to size the gland for the required O-ring compression.

    Blankoff attachment bolts

    1. Define the repeated load case

      I evaluated replacing the existing A280 mil-spec bolts with Grade 5 hardware stocked in the factory. I based the sizing on maximum feedline blow-off loads during cryo-proof testing.

    2. Calculate preload & torque

      From the blow-off force, I calculated the required bolt preload and tightening torque using T = KDF.

    3. Check gapping & fatigue

      I checked joint gapping and bolt fatigue under repeated loading. All checks passed, allowing us to replace the specialized bolts with standard Grade 5 hardware.

  5. 05

    Manufacturing & integration

    I released the revised parts, evaluated manufacturing deviations against critical sealing requirements, and oversaw welded-subassembly fabrication and vehicle integration.

  6. 06

    Additional considerations

    I rerouted drain tubes for work access and mistake-proofed an asymmetric subassembly. I maintained the 13,000-component CAD assembly across three vehicle release states and designed lineside storage to support two vehicles using the tools simultaneously.

V3 Starfactory Weld Cell & Integration Tooling

January – August 2025

I owned the CONOPS, layout, and tooling design for a V3 Starfactory cell building a ring manifold for oxygen, methane, environmental-control gases, and helium. I carried the work from fixture prototypes through machined tooling, lift planning, and vehicle integration.

Explore this project: Starship production tooling +

  1. 01

    Problem statement

    As SpaceX redesigned the factory line for Starship V3, I owned the CONOPS, layout, tooling design, and integration of a new build cell for a welded ring manifold of tubes extending 360° around the vehicle’s inner circumference.

  2. 02

    Design requirements

    Design the cell’s CONOPS for a target production rate of one completed assembly every 30 hours at full rate. Build tooling to clock critical tube endpoints within a 5° angular profile, with ±1 mm of translational freedom along the barrel’s inner circumference. Design a lift tool with a 1,100 lb working load limit and proof-test it at 2× WLL.

  3. 03

    Material selection

    I used PLA for printed fixture prototypes, then primarily aluminum for the machined fixtures to keep them light under modest loads. The lift tool used carbon steel with corrosion-resistant paint to prevent corrosion from spreading to flight hardware.

  4. 04

    Design & validation

    Weld cell & fixtures

    1. Define the locating constraints

      I used the Siegmund table holes to locate the fixtures and kept them clear of the Orbitalum orbital tube welder. The flanged endpoints needed precise position and clocking because they formed critical vehicle passthroughs.

    2. Size and tolerance the endpoint tools

      I used 3-2-1 locating principles, GD&T, and selected dowel-pin fits to size and tolerance the endpoint tools. I sized the tools’ seal glands using the Parker O-Ring Handbook.

    3. Prototype, learn, then machine

      I quickly 3D-printed PLA fixture components for the first article build, revised the designs from those fit-up lessons, and had predominantly aluminum tools machined for subsequent builds. I oversaw the cell’s setup and integration.

    Manifold lift tool

    1. Establish a three-point lift

      I worked with the assembly’s Responsible Engineer to establish rigging points on its brackets, then designed a three-point lift and rigging plan to fly the welded manifold into the vehicle.

    2. Size the structure and locate the lugs

      I used bending and deflection calculations to size the 1,100 lb WLL tool. I calculated bracket centers of gravity and placed welded lifting lugs directly above them to prevent tilt. I sized the lug welds so two lugs could carry the entire assembly.

    3. Prove the lift and plan installation

      I proof-tested the tool at 2,200 lb, twice its working load limit, and wrote the integration plan for welding the subassembly on the vehicle.

    Torque-frame access

    1. Define technician-applied loads

      I designed a stand to improve work access during aft flap torque-frame integration. I based its load cases on the maximum forces technicians could apply during assembly.

    2. Calculate bending, torsion, and weld loads

      I solved for two-plane bending and maximum torsion, then used those loads to size the integration stand and its welds.

    Flight-part retrofits

    1. Measure the as-built hardware

      I took precise measurements of existing assemblies and created as-built CAD models of the off-nominal conditions.

    2. Design parts to fit

      I used those models to design five flight-part retrofits for manufacture and installation on the existing assemblies.

  5. 05

    Additional considerations

    I also designed miscellaneous integration aids, including 3D-printed and Protolabs parts, to support the cell’s production process.

Brake Rotors & Mounts

2025 – 2026

I designed the rotor and mount for reliable braking within the drivetrain package, combining material tradeoffs, thermal analysis, structural FEA, and temperature testing.

Explore this project: Brake rotors & mounts +

  1. 01

    Problem statement

    I needed a reliable brake rotor and mount that fit the rear drivetrain package while limiting heat transfer into the mount.

  2. 02

    Design requirements

    Package the mount at the differential bolts, accommodate braking loads, and size the rotor for a 500°C temperature requirement with a safety margin.

  3. 03

    Material selection

    I compared cast iron and 4140 steel and selected steel for its endurance-limit advantage. Titanium buttons limited heat transfer into the 7075 aluminum mount, which I packaged at the differential bolts.

  4. 04

    Design & validation

    1. Thickness: bound and refine the heat load

      For the 500°C requirement, I first sized thickness assuming maximum braking energy became heat with no heat escaping. I refined that conservative estimate with transient conduction, forced convection, and radiation analysis.

    2. Geometry: manage debris and check stress

      I added curved face slots for debris removal, de-gassing, and water clearance, accepting additional pad wear. In Ansys, I supported the pad contact regions and applied braking moment through the mount connections to check stress.

  5. 05

    Additional considerations

    The face slots improved debris, gas, and water clearance at the cost of additional pad wear. Sponsorship availability drove the Brembo P2.34 caliper choice; the supporting matrix records the technical tradeoffs.

Calculations & reference material +

2026 FROZEN CALCULATIONS

From tire grip to rotor geometry

The rotor radius was part of a system-level balance: generate braking torque near each axle’s grip limit, then fit the pad and mounting buttons around that radius.

  1. 01 / Establish the braking load case

    The frozen model uses a 600 lbf vehicle-and-driver weight, 330 N of downforce, a 1,549.4 mm wheelbase and a 282.4 mm CG height. At 1.57 g, the recorded frontward load transfer is 763.9 N, leaving 2,263.3 N on the front axle and 735.6 N on the rear.

    Modeled longitudinal grip: 2,914.3 N front / 1,266.2 N rear.

  2. 02 / Match braking torque to available grip

    With a 100 lbf pedal input, 3.5:1 pedal ratio and 56.2% front bias, the model pairs 72 mm front and 80 mm rear effective rotor radii with 34 mm caliper pistons and a 0.50 pad friction coefficient. The resulting tire forces are 2,891.2 N front and 1,251.8 N rear.

    99.21% front / 98.87% rear of modeled locking force. Pedal-force gradient: 64.4 lbf/g, within the sheet’s 60–70 lbf/g target.

    Using axle torque = tire force × 0.1999 m loaded tire radius gives approximately 578.0 N·m front and 250.2 N·m rear. These are axle totals at the stated pedal input, not per-rotor ultimate loads.

  3. 03 / Turn effective radius into a rotor envelope

    The pad’s radial width establishes the swept band. Edge clearance sets the outer diameter; button size and button-to-pad clearance set the inner boundary. The frozen dimensions make those packaging allowances explicit.

    Frozen geometry, all dimensions in mm

    Effective radius 72.0 80.0

    The recorded dimensions reconcile with OD = 2 × effective radius + pad width + 2 × edge clearance, and ID = 2 × effective radius − pad width − 2 × button clearance − button diameter.

  4. 04 / Carry the envelope into detailed design

    This calculation establishes braking balance and radial packaging. Rotor thickness, heat transfer through the buttons, and rotor/carrier stresses require the separate thermal and structural checks described in the project. The frozen sheet does not establish those margins.

Rear Wheel Hub & Bearing Assembly

2024 – 2025

I designed a rear hub and bearing assembly that brought the drivetrain, wheel, and electronics interfaces together in a lightweight, manufacturable package.

Explore this project: Rear hub & bearing assembly +

  1. 01

    Problem statement

    I needed a lightweight rear hub that joined the drivetrain, wheel, bearing, and electronics interfaces while remaining practical to machine.

  2. 02

    Design requirements

    Accommodate the tripod bearing and Hall sensors, carry wheel and drivetrain loads, and package the bearing within an 80–85 mm bore and 110–120 mm outer-diameter envelope.

  3. 03

    Material selection

    I compared titanium, 6061, and 7075 aluminum and selected 7075-T6. Its higher yield strength than 6061 supported the hub’s structural requirements.

  4. 04

    Design & validation

    1. Select the bearing

      I screened bearings in SKF SimPro Quick for load capacity, fatigue life, and power loss. The selected bearing set the hub’s packaging and thickness requirements.

    2. Model the constrained geometry

      I built the initial hub geometry around the given packaging constraints and interface requirements from the drivetrain, wheel, bearing, and electronics systems. I sized hub-nut engagement using bolt tensile and nut thread-shear areas.

    3. Set up the FEA load cases

      In Ansys, I applied wheel loads from the team’s truss solver to the wheel flange, modeled the tensile load from the center-lock nut, and included bearing forces and drivetrain torque at the tripod interface.

    4. Optimize through pocketing

      I used the FEA results to identify where I could remove material, then iterated on the pockets and rechecked the revised hub in Ansys.

  5. 05

    Manufacturing & integration

    Rotational features were turned first; CNC milling then produced the tripod cutout, pin and button holes, and flange pockets.

  6. 06

    Additional considerations

    Bearing selection and machining access constrained where I could remove material. The selected SKF 71817 bearing is distinct from the 61916 pair documented in the supporting team-reference report.

Calculations & reference material +

BEARING SELECTION / PART OF THE HUB ASSEMBLY

Wheel bearing selection & analysis

I selected the SKF 71817 ACDGB/VQ253 angular-contact bearing after comparing candidates within the drivetrain packaging constraints. The SKF SimPro Quick study checked static and dynamic load capacity, fatigue life, and power loss across the operating wheel-speed range.

The selection saved 0.8 lb and established dimensions that fed into the hub design and thickness requirements. The simulation setup and power-loss plot are included in the project gallery.

Pushrods & Control Arms

2023 – 2024

I developed and fabricated a lighter suspension assembly, pairing carbon-fiber pushrods with steel control arms and checking both the bonded joints and on-car clearance.

Explore this project: Pushrods & control arms +

  1. 01

    Problem statement

    I redesigned and fabricated the control arms and pushrods to reduce suspension mass while preserving their interfaces and on-car clearance.

  2. 02

    Design requirements

    Connect the upright, chassis tabs, and pushrods; carry tension and compression loads; control the bonded end-plug fit; and maintain clearance through maximum steer.

  3. 03

    Material selection

    I retained steel control arms because the team had greater confidence in that manufacturing process. Carbon-fiber pushrods reduced mass, with turned aluminum end plugs providing the bonded connections.

  4. 04

    Design & validation

    1. Simplify the load introduction

      I replaced a heavy mounting plate with gussets at the control-arm apex.

    2. Test the pushrods

      I tested the carbon-fiber pushrods in tension and compression on an Instron.

  5. 05

    Manufacturing & integration

    I cut the carbon tubes and turned their end plugs, controlling bonding-surface diameter within 0.0005 in. For the control arms, I cut steel tubing, turned and milled plugs, bored and reamed bearing seats, and welded and tapped rod-end interfaces.

  6. 06

    Additional considerations

    During freshman-year integration, a missed tolerance stackup caused the apex to interfere at maximum steer. We checked the required material removal in Ansys and ground the existing apex to recover clearance. The combined suspension redesign saved 4.6 lb.

Discrete Ride-Height Adjustment

2023 – 2024

I designed a shim-based attachment to make ride-height changes more repeatable, replacing a process that relied on turning an opposite-thread pushrod.

Explore this project: Discrete ride-height adjustment +

  1. 01

    Problem statement

    Ride-height adjustment relied on turning an opposite-thread pushrod. I designed a shim-based attachment for more repeatable setup changes.

  2. 02

    Design requirements

    Change pushrod length in discrete increments, retain the inboard suspension interface, and keep the shim stack in compression under pushrod loading.

  3. 03

    Material selection

    I used 7075 aluminum for the attachment and minimized shim mass through lightweight geometry.

  4. 04

    Design & validation

    1. Define the adjustment mechanism

      Adding or removing shims changes pushrod length; retightening the fasteners secures the setting. I kept the shim pockets concentric with the retaining bolts.

  5. 05

    Manufacturing & integration

    The shims were laser-cut and the attachment pieces machined. Another team member handled the upper component’s CNC machining.

  6. 06

    Additional considerations

    The mechanism added 0.7 lb across the car in exchange for more repeatable ride-height adjustment.
